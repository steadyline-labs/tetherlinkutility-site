Opinion

Nobody Reads the Contract. That's the Point.

Published September 22, 2026 · Updated September 25, 2026

You sign up for "unlimited." You pay for it every month without thinking twice. Then one day you plug your laptop into your phone's hotspot because you need to get real work done, and the connection crawls to a stop. It turns out your unlimited data has a separate, much smaller cap the moment it leaves your phone, a quota you never agreed to in any sentence you actually read.

That money wasn't free. It's what's left over after real work, real hours, and we hand it over every month for one simple reason: we want to stay connected to the world. That's the entire point of paying for "unlimited" in the first place, not a bonus feature. So when the same operators we trust with that money quietly cap the very thing we paid to be unlimited, using a clause buried three pages deep, it doesn't feel like a technicality. It feels like being shortchanged for wanting to stay in touch.

This isn't one company, one country, or one bad month. It's a pattern, and it's a pattern by design.

The terms that matter are the ones you never see

Nobody sits down and reads forty pages of tariff terms before buying a phone plan. Providers know this. So the parts that would make you hesitate, like a hotspot allowance that's a fraction of your main data, or a clause that quietly limits your right to switch to a better plan, don't get put on the page you actually look at. They get put on the page you don't.

That's not an accident of legal writing. A term that only protects the seller and never gets shown to the buyer isn't a boring technicality, it's a choice. If a restriction is important enough to change what I actually get for my money, it belongs in plain language, on the page where I make the decision, not three menus deep in an app or buried in a PDF nobody opens.

I'm not arguing that companies can't set limits. Any business can decide what it sells. I'm arguing that a limit hidden well enough that you only discover it when you hit it isn't really a limit you agreed to. It's a limit you were exposed to.

This is already a global fight, not just mine

I used to think this was just a local frustration. It isn't. Regulators in very different markets have already looked at the same pattern and pushed back on it.

  • European Union: under EU telecom rules, a provider that changes your contract terms has to give at least a month's notice, and if the change doesn't benefit you, you get the right to walk away without penalty. The EU's Consumer Rights Directive also gives a 14-day no-questions-asked cancellation window on online subscriptions.
  • India: the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) requires tariff terms and restrictions to be disclosed transparently before purchase, and its draft Telecom Consumer Protection Regulation, 2026 is specifically aimed at tightening how clearly plans and their limits have to be communicated.
  • Brazil: ANATEL's consumer rules require every charge on a bill to be itemized with its basis, mandate that offers show price, benefits, and restrictions up front, and back that up with fines that have run into the tens of millions of reais for operators that don't comply.
  • United States: the FTC has treated buried limitations and cancellation obstacles as illegal "dark patterns," most visibly in a $100 million action against Vonage over junk fees and hard-to-cancel service, and in a joint action with several states against Frontier Communications over internet speeds that didn't match what customers were sold.

Different regulators, different legal systems, same underlying complaint: customers were sold one thing in the headline and given another thing in the footnote. That's not a coincidence. It means enough people, in enough places, said the same thing loudly enough that someone with actual authority had to respond.

What I actually want changed

Not free service. Not the end of tiered plans, tiers are fine, clearly labeled limits are fine. What I want is simple: if a term restricts what I'm getting, or restricts my ability to leave for something better, it has to be visible before I pay, not just discoverable after I complain. A right to switch plans shouldn't be easier to find in a regulator's ruling than in the contract itself.

What I did about it, for myself

I couldn't rewrite anyone's terms of service. So I built the part I could control. Tether Link Utility is a small, free Android app that shares your phone's mobile data with your other devices through a local proxy, without routing anything through a remote server or asking for an account. It doesn't change your plan or its terms: data used by connected devices still counts toward your plan, so it is worth checking your carrier's tethering terms, and the clearer those terms are, the better for everyone.

What regulators in Brussels, Delhi, Brasilia, and Washington have been pushing for is simple: customers should be able to see the real terms before they pay. Until that is the norm everywhere, the most useful habit is to open your plan details and look for the hotspot line before you rely on it.

I know how easy it would be to wave this off as marketing dressed up as outrage. So let me be plain about the difference. The operators charging you for "unlimited" and then metering it in the footnotes are worth tens of billions of dollars. This app isn't. Tether Link Utility asks for a small subscription for one reason: to keep this worth building and maintaining. There's no fixed number where that flips. But the day ads alone make it worth sustaining on their own, the subscription goes away. Not a discount, not a promotion, gone, because at that point we won't need it anymore.
